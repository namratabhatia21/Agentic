# Why context changes everything

## Why this matters

Two people can type almost the same request and get very different answers. The
difference is nearly always context: what the assistant knows about the situation.

**By the end of this lesson you can** explain how added context and examples change an
assistant's answer.

## Start from what you know

In the last lesson you met the four parts of a prompt. This lesson looks closely at the
two that people skip most often: context and examples.

## What context does

An assistant has to decide what to include, what to leave out, and what tone to use.
Without context it picks the most common answer for that kind of request. Context
narrows the choice:

- **Audience** decides vocabulary and depth ("for a new intern" vs. "for the legal team").
- **Purpose** decides what matters ("to decide whether to renew" vs. "to celebrate").
- **Constraints** rule options out ("we can't change the deadline").

## What examples do

An example shows tone, length and structure all at once, so you don't have to describe
them. One good example often does more than a paragraph of instructions. Make sure the
example is one you'd be happy to receive, because the assistant will copy its flaws too.

## Side by side

| Prompt | What the assistant has to guess |
|---|---|
| "Summarize this report." | Reader, length, focus, tone |
| "Summarize this report for our CFO." | Length, focus, tone |
| "Summarize this report for our CFO, who cares about cost. 5 bullets." | Tone |
| ...plus "Here's last quarter's summary as a model." | Almost nothing |

## Try it

Your prompt "Write a LinkedIn post about our new office" produced something stiff and
corporate. Which single addition would most likely fix the tone?

- An example of a past post you liked. It shows the tone directly. "Make it casual" helps
  too, but people mean different things by "casual"; an example removes the doubt.

## Key takeaways

- Without context, the assistant falls back on the most common answer.
- Audience, purpose and constraints are the context that changes answers most.
- An example communicates tone, length and structure in one go, flaws included.
